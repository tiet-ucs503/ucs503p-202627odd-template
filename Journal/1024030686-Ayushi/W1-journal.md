# W1 — Understanding the Problem and Defining CityServe

## Objective

The objective for the first week was to understand the problem we wanted to solve, identify our target users, and decide what CityServe should actually offer instead of immediately jumping into development.

## Work Done

During the first week, we explored the idea of building a platform that connects people with local service providers. We discussed several everyday services that students and other people in a local community may need, such as electricians, mechanics, tutors, tailors, laundry services, salons, photographers and other small businesses.

At first, we were thinking mainly about creating a platform where users could search for nearby vendors. However, while discussing the idea from a user's perspective, we realised that a simple list of vendors would not solve the complete problem. A user may find a vendor but would still have questions such as whether the vendor is trustworthy, how much the service will cost, whether they are currently available, and what other customers think about them.

Because of this, we started shaping CityServe as more than just a vendor directory. The idea gradually developed into a local-services marketplace where users can discover providers, compare their options and eventually interact with them through the platform.

We identified the main user journey as:

**Discover → Search → Compare → Book/Order → Pay → Track → Review**

This helped us understand that the platform would need to support the complete service experience rather than only helping users find a vendor.

## Defining the Initial Market

Another important discussion during the week was deciding where CityServe should initially operate.

Instead of trying to target an entire city from the beginning, we considered starting around the **Thapar ecosystem and nearby areas**. This gives us a smaller and more accessible user group consisting primarily of students, faculty and staff. It also makes it easier for us to understand the kinds of services people around us actually need.

Starting locally also gives us an opportunity to validate the concept before considering expansion to a larger geographical area.

## Identifying the Trust Problem

While defining the platform, we realised that trust would be one of the biggest challenges.

Local service providers are often discovered through personal recommendations, informal contacts or word of mouth. A new user may have very little information about a vendor before deciding to hire them. Therefore, simply bringing vendors and customers onto the same platform would not automatically make the marketplace useful.

We discussed features that could help address this issue, including:

* Verification of service providers
* Vendor profiles with relevant information
* Ratings and customer reviews
* Transparent pricing
* Availability information
* Booking and order status
* Service/order tracking
* Customer feedback
* Administrative monitoring

These discussions helped us realise that reliability should be considered as part of the product itself rather than something added later.

## Key Learning

The biggest learning from this week was that we should not start by deciding what technologies or features we want to build. We first need to understand the actual problem and the people experiencing it.

We also learned that there is a difference between a **vendor directory** and a **marketplace**. A directory mainly helps users discover businesses, whereas a marketplace can support the interaction that follows discovery, such as booking, payment, tracking and feedback.

For CityServe, our current direction is therefore to build a trusted local-services marketplace, initially focused on the Thapar ecosystem and surrounding areas, and validate whether it can genuinely make finding and using local services easier for users.
